Most patios on Long Island are built from one of three materials: concrete pavers, poured concrete or natural stone such as bluestone and flagstone. All three look good on the day they're finished. The difference shows up after a few winters, and in how easy the patio is to fix when something moves.

## The short version

**Concrete pavers**

- **Look:** many shapes, colors and patterns.
- **Freeze and thaw:** joints let the surface shift without cracking.
- **Repairs:** lift and reset the pieces that moved.
- **Upfront cost:** usually more than plain concrete.
- **Upkeep:** top up joint sand, pull the odd weed.

**Poured concrete**

- **Look:** plain, brushed or stamped.
- **Freeze and thaw:** one slab, so ground movement shows up as cracks.
- **Repairs:** patches rarely match the rest.
- **Upfront cost:** usually the lowest.
- **Upkeep:** reseal stamped concrete every few years.

**Natural stone**

- **Look:** natural color and texture, no two pieces alike.
- **Freeze and thaw:** dry-laid stone moves like pavers; mortared joints can crack.
- **Repairs:** reset single stones; matching color can take time.
- **Upfront cost:** usually the highest.
- **Upkeep:** top up joints; some stone needs sealing.

## Why winter decides it

Long Island winters swing above and below freezing again and again. Water that soaks into the ground or into a crack freezes, expands and pushes. A solid slab can't flex, so the stress comes out as a crack. Pavers and dry-laid stone are separate pieces on a compacted base, so they can shift a little and settle back without breaking.

That doesn't make pavers indestructible. Pavers on a poor base sink and heave too. The difference is the fix: you lift the low area, rebuild the base and relay the same pavers.

## What really makes a patio last

When a patio sinks, rocks or holds puddles, the cause is almost always underneath it, not the material on top. A patio that lasts has:

- **Proper excavation.** Topsoil and roots come out. They compress and rot, and anything built on them settles.
- **A compacted gravel base.** Crushed stone goes down in layers, each one compacted with a plate compactor. A patio for chairs and a grill needs less base than a driveway that carries cars.
- **A slight pitch away from the house.** Commonly about 1/8 to 1/4 inch per foot, so rain runs off instead of pooling against the foundation.
- **Edge restraint.** Pavers need a solid edge or the outer rows creep outward over the years.
- **Joint sand.** Polymeric sand locks pavers together and slows down weeds and ants.

Around Patchogue the soil is mostly sand and sandy loam that drains well, which helps any patio. Yards near the bay and creeks can be wetter, and lots that were filled during construction can hold water near the surface. Those need more attention to the base and to where the water goes.

If water already pools where you want the patio, fix that first. Our guide to [dry wells and French drains](/blog/dry-well-vs-french-drain-long-island) covers the options.

## Pavers: the most flexible choice

Pavers are what most homeowners pick, and most of the patios in [our work gallery](/our-work) are pavers. Why:

- **Choice.** Dozens of colors, sizes and textures, laid in patterns like herringbone, running bond or a two-color checkerboard.
- **No curing time.** A paver patio can be used as soon as the joints are filled. Poured concrete needs time to cure first.
- **Matching extras.** Seating walls, steps and fire pits can be built from block that matches the patio.
- **Easy repairs.** If a tree root or a washout lifts one area, only that area gets reset.

The downsides: joint sand washes out slowly and needs topping up, weeds can root in joints that are left open, and pavers cost more upfront than plain concrete.

## Poured concrete: lowest upfront, hardest to fix

Concrete can be left smooth, brushed for grip, or stamped and colored to look like stone. For a simple rectangle on a tight budget it's hard to beat on price.

The trade-off is cracking. Control joints help cracks form in straight lines where they're less noticeable, but a slab will still crack as the ground moves. Patches rarely match the color and texture around them, and stamped concrete needs resealing to keep its color. When a slab fails, the usual fix is to break it out and pour again.

## Natural stone: bluestone and flagstone

Bluestone comes cut into rectangles or in irregular pieces. Flagstone is irregular, with every piece a different shape. Both give a patio a natural look that pavers can only imitate.

Stone is either **dry-laid** on gravel and stone dust, or **set in mortar** on a concrete slab. Dry-laid stone behaves like pavers in winter. Mortared stone looks very clean, but its joints and the slab under it can crack with freeze and thaw, like any concrete.

A few things to know before choosing stone:

- Dark stone such as bluestone gets hot in direct summer sun. Keep that in mind around a pool.
- It's usually the most expensive option, and irregular pieces take longer to fit.
- It mixes well with other materials. A flagstone path set in pea gravel with a granite block edge, for example, gives a natural look with clean lines.

## What changes the price

We can't give a fair price without seeing the yard, because the material is only part of the cost. The biggest factors are:

- **Size and shape.** Curves and patterns mean more cutting.
- **Access.** Whether a machine can reach the backyard or everything goes in by wheelbarrow.
- **Removal.** Breaking out an old patio, a slab or a deck.
- **Grading and base.** How much soil comes out, how much gravel goes in, and whether the yard slopes.
- **Material and pattern.** Pavers, concrete or stone, and how intricate the layout is.
- **Extras.** Seating walls, steps, a fire pit, lighting or drainage.

## Do you need a permit?

It depends on where you live and what's being built. The Town of Brookhaven and incorporated villages such as Patchogue each have their own building departments and rules. Retaining walls are listed on Brookhaven's building permit application, so if your patio needs a wall to hold back a slope, ask before work starts: Town of Brookhaven Building Division at (631) 451-8696, or your village's building department.

## When to build

Patios can be built any time the ground isn't frozen. Spring is the busiest season for landscapers, so if you want a patio ready for summer, start planning in late winter. That leaves time to choose materials and get on the schedule.
