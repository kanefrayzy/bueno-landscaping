## The short version

| Compare | Dry well | French drain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | A buried pit or chamber that holds storm water and lets it soak into the ground | A gravel-filled trench with a perforated pipe that collects water and carries it away |
| Best for | Roof water from downspouts, runoff from a driveway or patio, one low spot | A strip of soggy lawn, water moving toward the house, a wet area along a slope or fence |
| Where the water goes | Down, into the soil | Sideways, to an outlet such as a dry well |
| What it needs | Soil that takes in water, above the groundwater | A steady slope to the outlet |

Many yards need both: the French drain collects the water, and the dry well gives it somewhere to go.

## How a dry well works

A dry well is a hole dug well below the surface and fitted with a precast concrete or plastic chamber, or filled with stone. Pipe from downspouts or from a drain runs into it. During a storm it fills faster than it empties, then lets the water soak into the surrounding soil over the next hours.

A dry well works when:

- the soil around it is sandy or gravelly and takes water in,
- its bottom sits above the groundwater,
- it's sized for the roof or paved area that drains into it,
- it's placed away from the house foundation and from any septic system or cesspool.

## How a French drain works

A French drain is a trench, usually lined with filter fabric, with a perforated pipe on a bed of gravel and more gravel on top. Water in the soil and on the surface seeps into the gravel, enters the pipe and flows downhill to an outlet. The trench has to slope the whole way, or water just sits in the pipe.

It's the right tool when water spreads across an area or moves through the ground. If the water comes from one downspout, a French drain is more than you need.

## What Long Island soil means for drainage

USDA soil maps for the Patchogue area show mostly sandy soils: Plymouth loamy coarse sand, rated excessively drained, and Riverhead sandy loam, rated well drained. That's good news for dry wells. In most yards around here, water soaks in quickly once it gets below the topsoil.

So why do yards still flood? Usually because of what's on top:

- **Compacted soil or fill** left from construction, which water can't get through.
- **Downspouts** dumping a whole roof's worth of water in one spot next to the foundation.
- **Grading** that tips the lawn or patio toward the house.
- **Low areas near the bay and creeks**, where the soils are wetter and the groundwater is closer to the surface. There a dry well can end up sitting in groundwater and stop draining, so the fix is often regrading or sending the water somewhere else.

## Which one do you need?

- **Water pools at the bottom of a downspout or splashes against the foundation:** pipe the downspout underground to a dry well away from the house.
- **A strip of lawn stays soggy for days:** a French drain along the wet area, emptying into a dry well.
- **Water runs across the yard toward the house:** regrade so the ground slopes away, sometimes with a French drain to catch what's left.
- **Puddles on a patio or driveway:** check the pitch first, then add a channel drain or a dry well at the low edge. Our guide to [patio materials](/blog/paver-vs-concrete-vs-stone-patio) covers why pitch matters.

Most drainage problems are a mix, so the first step is watching where the water comes from and where it collects during a heavy rain. Photos or a short video taken during a storm help a lot when we look at a yard.

## Before you dig

- **Call 811** a few days before digging so buried gas, electric and water lines get marked. It's free.
- **Ask about permits.** The Town of Brookhaven's grading code calls for dry wells where roof runoff would cause erosion or drainage problems for neighbors, and work near wetlands has its own rules. Whether your project needs a permit depends on where you live and what you're changing, so check first: the Town of Brookhaven Building Division at (631) 451-8696, or the building department of your village if you live in one, such as Patchogue (Village Hall, (631) 475-4300).
- **Keep the water on your property.** Sending it onto a neighbor's yard moves the problem instead of fixing it.
